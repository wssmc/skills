# Prose Guidance

## 1. Prefer direct scientific statements

Use verified evidence directly.

Prefer:

> `Method A achieves the lowest ARPD on the Large benchmark.`

Avoid unnecessary meta-language:

> `Based on the currently available evidence, Method A appears to possibly achieve...`

Truthfulness is not the same as excessive hedging.

## 2. Keep one scientific function per sentence when possible

Long sentences are acceptable when clause relationships are necessary, but avoid stacking background, method, result, and caveat into one sentence.

## 3. Vary sentence structure without sacrificing precision

Use a mixture of:
- direct subject–verb sentences;
- contrastive sentences;
- evidence-led sentences;
- causal/conditional sentences;
- short synthesis sentences.

Do not create artificial variation by replacing precise technical terms with synonyms.

## 4. Reduce formulaic transitions

Avoid repetitive use of:
- `Moreover,`
- `Furthermore,`
- `In addition,`
- `It is worth noting that...`

Prefer semantic transitions where the sentence itself shows the relation.

## 5. Control nominalization and passive voice

Use passive voice when the process/result matters more than the actor. Use active voice when the scientific action is clearer.

Prefer:

> `The decoder assigns each operation to the earliest-start machine.`

when this is clearer than:

> `The assignment of each operation is performed by the decoder...`

## 6. Preserve terminology

Once a formal term is defined, reuse it consistently. Do not alternate between:
- `job` / `order` / `task`;
- `release time` / `availability time`;
- `best solution` / `elite solution` / `incumbent`;

unless they are genuinely different objects.

Use the project terminology glossary when available.

## 7. Avoid unsupported rhetorical language

Do not use:
- `remarkable`;
- `dramatic`;
- `excellent`;
- `superior`;
- `robust`;
- `state-of-the-art`;

unless the evidence and context justify the specific claim.

## 8. Do not repeat limitations everywhere

A scope limitation should appear where it changes interpretation. It does not need to be restated after every result.

Overclaiming/underclaiming is audited in `checks/full-manuscript-check.md`.

## 9. Avoid dash-heavy and AI-like prose

In the final manuscript, do not use hyphens, em dashes, en dashes, or dash-based fragments as a substitute for ordinary syntax. Prefer complete clauses, conjunctions, commas, semicolons, or separate sentences. This is especially important for long chains that compress problem, method, evidence, and conclusion into a slogan-like expression.

Do not mechanically remove symbols that carry technical meaning. Preserve:

- established technical compounds whose hyphenation is conventional;
- mathematical minus signs and operators;
- numerical ranges and interval notation;
- citations, identifiers, file names, and formal labels;
- punctuation required by the target journal or language.

During polishing, inspect repeated dash patterns and rewrite only gratuitous uses. The goal is natural academic syntax, not zero hyphens.
